Nordson — the cultivated library

An image library built and illustrated for drop-in reuse across a product line. One canonical subject, two rendered forms — and an illustration is the infographic of a photograph.

Design. The machine as geometry — line, on the grid, with no lighting and no room around it. I worked with the engineers on this machine's form and drew its mark, so the object and its identity were settled together. Held as line, it can be drawn again at any size for a schematic, a cutaway or a wall.

Deploy. The machine as it ships — shot, composited and retouched into a drop-in asset that lands in a spec sheet, a page or a trade-show graphic without anyone opening a camera or a file again.

Content systems

As a consequence

Once the geometry exists, the renderings are free

The wipe above is design to deploy. This is what follows from it — a finished trade ad, and not one hour of it was photography. No shoot, no studio, no retouch, because the machine was already held as line.

That is the argument for a library, and it is not that any single image is good. It is that the next request is already answered — a spec sheet on Tuesday, a trade-show wall on Friday, both out of the same geometry, neither of them a project.

Trade advertisement · Nordson Industrial Coating Systems

Why bother

An illustration is the infographic of a photograph

A photograph tells you what a machine looks like. A drawing tells you what it is — it discards the reflections and the factory floor and keeps the form, the parts, and the relationships. One is evidence, the other is explanation, and an industrial line needs both constantly.

Holding both for every unit means the request "we need the Cordis for a spec sheet by Friday" stops being a project. The asset already exists, in the right form, at the right size, cut out, on the right background. That is the whole argument: the library is the deliverable, not any single image in it.

ShowDesk

A trade-show application that builds a show out of the library above. Two clips at a time — product and text — laid into one frame in four layouts, with transitions, in either orientation. It runs off a USB stick on a booth PC with no network and no database, and it exists because the assets were built to be dropped in.

The Show Builder, loaded with PaintExpo 2026 — the tool, not a show. The preview is in Even: two clips, product and text, in one frame. That is the Cordis from the piece above, arriving here as a clip.

Environmental and trade shows

Why it exists

The library's payoff, not a second project

The piece above claims the library was built for drop-in reuse. This is the thing that took it at its word. A booth needs a different show for every event, assembled by whoever is standing in the booth, and that is only possible if every asset is already the right form at the right size on the right ground. ShowDesk could be written because the answer to "what do we have" was a folder rather than a request.

The separation is visible in the picture above: the Cordis from the piece before, arriving here as a clip in a list. The footage belongs to Nordson and the machine that arranges it is mine — and so are the modules, which is the part that makes the rest of it work. Drop different files in those folders and it is a different show without a line changing.

The modules

The filename is the whole schema

I make the clips as well as the thing that plays them — Cordis, Encore VT & HD, Dynamic Contouring, Color On Demand, the alerts reel, the applications parade. That is the video half of this piece, and it is why the two halves fit together: there is no import step and no database between them, only a naming convention.

A module is one prefix. Cordis_product.mp4 and Cordis_text.mp4 become a paired module the builder can lay side by side, stack, or run either half full frame; Cordis_full.mp4 is a single. Anything that matches nothing is treated as full frame, so a file dropped in by someone who has never read this still plays.

Authoring the content and the tool together is what lets the seam between them be a filename. Neither half had to be told about the other.

How it is built

A booth appliance made of a browser

About 7,600 lines of JavaScript and CSS, hand-written, no framework. A .bat file starts a small PowerShell static server and launches Chrome in kiosk mode against it; the app reads video, audio and PDFs straight off disk through the File System Access API. No network calls, no telemetry, no upload step and no database — the media folders are the library.

It goes through localhost rather than file:// for two reasons that are the same reason: File System Access needs a secure context, and the folder permission Chrome persists is scoped per origin. Anchor everything to one port and the field tech picks the folder once, ever. That is the kind of decision this whole page is about — invisible when right, and a support call every morning of the show when wrong.

The handoff

A tool nobody can run is not a tool

It shipped with three documents, and they are the half of this that is rarer than the code. A staff guide for booth operators — where the hidden panel is, how to interrupt the loop for a customer, why the USB connector should be blue. A hardware list with two setups, touchscreen or presenter remote, and a pre-show checklist that opens by warning that Smart App Control is a one-way switch the customer has to agree to first. And an orientation note to the colleague installing it, explaining which part calls which and why, before she reads any of the detail.

Anyone can say they built a tool. The evidence that it was built for other people to run is the note to the person who ran it.

Ascend

A creative operating environment inside a Fortune 500 manufacturer. Intake, language, archive, and the portal that composes them.

Stage of the pipeline

01 · ArtStart — intake

Requests arrive against real delivery formats, so a job is born already tied to its size constraints, its editorial calendar, and its localisation needs. ArtStart also does the administrative labour nobody wants: notifying stakeholders, trafficking work across teams, and building standardised working files on the operator's own machine. By the time a job reaches a desk, the only thing left to do is the creative part.

02 · Copydesk — language

Writing sits on the production schedule rather than beside it, and copy is reviewed in its eventual formatting instead of as loose text — so everyone sees how language will actually land before anything ships. Every revision is archived structurally, which turns the copy itself into an asset: the same database feeds product collateral, documentation, and localisation, and generates the translation surfaces as a by-product.

03 · Codedesk — codes

QR codes treated as system components rather than marketing artifacts. Types are structured, styling is consistent per category, and the emoji that marks a code is also the source of its palette — so a code signals its intent before anyone scans it. Same engine as the public product; only the manifest differs.

04 · FileRoom — archive

Finished work lands with its context intact: the original request, the specs, the versions, the approvals and the deliverables stay linked as one record. Alongside that sits a growing library of prepared elements — images, layouts, production-ready components — so the expensive parts stay findable instead of being rebuilt from memory.

05 · The portal — composed

None of the four know about each other. Each publishes one canonical artifact and the portal composes them, which is why any one of them can change without the others noticing. That decoupling is the reason the thing survived contact with a real organisation — and it is the same structure I have since built, alone, for a neighbourhood.

Creative operations

Why it holds

Design, writing and technical work as one system

Ascend was built where creative work is not the main event: a large industrial manufacturer, where integrated teams had to move fast and stay aligned without losing context, institutional knowledge, or taste. The usual failure there is not bad work but lost work — decisions made twice, assets rebuilt, language drifting between documents.

Treating ideas, assets and decisions as one connected record from intake to archive is what stops that. It is unglamorous, and it is the thing that actually raises what a team can ship.

The QR engine

A QR code is a piece of public infrastructure, and people should be able to make and click free QRs without hydrating someone else's marketing program with hidden extra URL data. The expression is if there's no product you're the product — but that's not true at the okQRal.

Live at okqral.com

Systems design

Why it holds

Different on purpose, from one spine

A public tool and an internal one usually diverge into two codebases, then drift, and then one of them quietly dies. These three stay different on purpose. The public build carries the full type vocabulary because strangers arrive wanting anything; the Nordson build carries only what Nordson uses, because a shorter list is a faster tool for people doing one job all day; and The Ward's is embedded inside a product about neighbourhoods, where a code is a detail rather than the point.

None of that is three codebases. It is one engine and three manifests — so each deployment can be shaped to its own job without any of them drifting away from the others.

The split version is larger in total, not smaller — five engines come to about twice the monolith. That is the honest trade. What it buys is that a change to rendering cannot break state, and a change to state cannot break sync, which is what lets one engine survive three owners.

The emoji

The mark is also the palette

Most styled QR codes bolt an icon onto a black square. Here the emoji you pick is run through a colour extractor and becomes the code's palette — so one choice sets both what the code means and what it looks like. A code signals its category before anyone scans it, and it does so without a separate design decision.

Codes are treated as system components rather than marketing artifacts: structured types, consistent visual systems per category, and PNG output so they render the same everywhere. They can be regenerated without changing what they encode.

The Ward

The commons, rendered as the actual place. Two authoring tools pour into a slab; a player animates it and carries the ticker and the bulletin board. Three neighbourhoods, one kit.

Which layer of The Ward to load

The slab

The environment. Ground, trees, weather and sky — authored by three separate tools and composed at runtime.

The Ward

The commons. Ticker, bulletin board, place cards, presence — an interface layer that lifts off the environment cleanly, so it can stand on a sheet of its own.

The Ward, running, served from its own address with nothing added for this page. The switcher above chooses which payload loads — the slab alone, both, or the commons alone — and each arrives from a separate URL. The slab underneath was baked by a different tool than the one drawing it.

Platform architecture

The slab

Ground, canopy, weather

Cartograph publishes the ground as a single drawing per Look. Arborist keeps a species library and bakes it to instanced geometry — per-specimen jitter, bark, and seasons, so a street of the same species is not a street of clones. Weather and sky run on a poller and a timeline rather than a fixed skybox, which means the neighbourhood has hours and conditions instead of a single flattering afternoon.

The Gateway Arch is built from the real NPS catenary equation rather than eyeballed, and it sits in the scene's own coordinate space instead of being painted on the horizon — so buildings occlude its legs, and gaps between them do not.

The Ward

A noticeboard inside the place it describes

Most neighbourhood software is a feed with an address attached. The Ward puts the commons where the thing actually is: an event on the ticker, a notice on the board, a business as a card you meet by walking past it. Because the whole layer is DOM rather than geometry, it is responsive, legible, and separable — which is why it can be shown on its own sheet without the environment beneath it.

Why it matters

One kit, three neighbourhoods

Looks vary styling and never shape, so the runtime swaps between them at no cost — a different palette and ground over identical geometry. That rule is what turns one neighbourhood into a kit, and the kit is already real: Lafayette Square built out to full fidelity, Highpointe Demun close behind, Altadena with mountains and twenty thousand people.

Each helper publishes exactly one canonical artifact and never imports another's code. It is the same structure I built at Nordson and, before that, for a tourism guild — arrived at again, alone, for a different problem.

Retouching and compositing

How it works

A catalogue needs more rooms than a budget has

A seasonal range has to appear in a plausible home, in enough different homes that the range reads as a range. Building or booking a location for every configuration is the expensive way there, and it locks you into whatever you shot on the day.

Shooting the location empty, once, changes the arithmetic. The plate is captured on site with the real light. The objects are shot separately in a studio where they can be lit and re-lit and reshot. What arrives at the end is a photograph nobody took.

It is not a beauty pass. Sun direction and shadow length have to agree with a plate shot hours earlier or on another day, and contact shadows have to be built. In pair 8 the wall is extended to carry a third window that was never there; in pair 1 a blown-out window is replaced with real foliage and the room gains a cactus, a basket, and a bench.

Once a plate exists it can be reused. That is the argument: the marginal cost of the next configuration falls to studio time, and the location is available forever.

Picture Wrap

Every picture wraps twice: first when the shooting stops, and finally when the last person who made it is gone. A film's page is one list of everyone credited on it, divided by a gold bar — the living above, the dead below. As people die the bar rises. When it reaches the top the picture has wrapped, and it enters the Vault.

Live at picture-wrap.com · posting closed pictures at @picture-wrap.social

Data and archives

The display is the invention

One bar carries the whole state

The interesting problem was never fetching the data. It was finding a way to show mortality across a cast and crew of hundreds without turning it into a table nobody reads. One horizontal rule does it: everyone above is alive, everyone below is not, and the rule's height is the number. You read the state of a film before you read a single name.

It also means the graphic updates itself. Nothing needs redrawing when someone dies — the bar simply sits higher than it did, and eventually reaches the top.

How it is built

Two halves that barely know each other

The site is static files. No backend, no build step; the browser queries Wikidata and TMDB directly. The poster is a set of Node scripts that finds pictures which have closed, holds them for approval, and posts the approved ones to Bluesky. Delete the poster and the site still works — it just has no Vault.

They share exactly two files, and the difference between those two is the whole design. shared.js is what both halves must agree about — properties, languages, pure helpers. verify.js is what they must agree on: whether anyone who made a picture is still alive.

The poster writes a 4.5 MB archive for itself and a sharded vault for the browser — a summary, an index of ids, and one file per decade. The site never fetches the archive; at that size it would be a poor thing to hand somebody who only wanted the front page.

Scale Machine

A trumpet's C sounds a concert B♭. An alto's sounds E♭. Tell four players a tune is in A minor and only the pianist can use it. Scale Machine holds one canonical set of scales and re-spells them for whichever horn you play — twelve keys, engraved, right clef, right key signature. Four players read four different pages and one sound comes out.

Live at scalemachine.app · no account, and the microphone never leaves the browser

Music and notation

Why it is here

Transposition is compositing

Everything else on this page composites in space — a plate over a photograph, a slab under a commons, a studio shot into an empty room. This one composites in sound, and it is the clearest case because the sources cannot be reconciled by looking.

The seven notes are the same seven notes. What changes is the page each player reads. Four musicians hold four different documents, disagreeing about the name of every note in front of them, and the disagreement is exactly what makes them agree out loud. That is the whole thesis at the smallest size it can be seen at.

How it is built

One thing that is true, one thing that draws it

core/theory.js is pure — no DOM, no window, no knowledge that a browser exists. It spells scales and transposes them, and that is all it does. core/engrave.js takes what it says and draws staves. Delete the drawing and the theory is untouched; the split is the same one every other piece here argues for, at a size where you can hold both halves in your head at once.

No backend, no build step, no account. The listener runs on the microphone in the browser and nothing it hears is recorded, uploaded or kept — which is a design decision, not a privacy notice: there is nowhere for it to go.

Not built yet

A second reader for the same core

The point of a pure core is that more than one thing can read it. A key-resolution service and a daily post to Bluesky are specified and not written — so today the core has exactly one reader, and the claim above is a structure rather than a demonstration. When the second lands it will be said here plainly, and not before.
