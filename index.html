Ascend Visualization Studio

A canonical library of modular creative assets, and the tools to put them to work across teams, channels, and markets.

Structure. I worked with the engineers on the Next Generation Feed Center — the programme that became the Cordis.

Expression. I made the final assets and carried them through production, publication, and printing.

Nordson makes complex engineered products for markets around the world.

A cross-division product-development programme brought the practice together; Cordis was the product that emerged from it.

The Studio

Nordson needs a lot of content, in a lot of formats, for a lot of purposes, and that content has to remain accurate and usable as it moves through the organization.

I built Ascend Visualization Studio around a canonical library of modular creative assets: product imagery, illustration, layouts, templates, and production-ready components that can be assembled for different applications without recreating the underlying work each time.

The objective is straightforward: do the operational work ahead of time, make it durable, and leave more time for the work itself — creative judgment, careful production, and the precision required to represent complex engineered products correctly.

Ascend Toolkit

A creative operating system that brings producers, writers, designers, and developers into the same workflow.

Stage of the pipeline

01 · ArtStart — intake

A workspace that turns a work order into a complete creative brief for the artist.

The system assembles the specifications, delivery requirements, copy, imagery, QR code, and workspace link, then handles the handoff through final delivery.

02 · CopyDesk — language

A collaborative writing workspace for long-form content such as specification sheets and international marketing materials.

The team edits one source document together; the system translates it into the required languages and routes each version to a human translator for review.

03 · CodeDesk — codes

A QR code generator for creative projects and connected experiences.

CodeDesk creates and manages the QR codes used to connect campaigns, environments, and other physical experiences to digital content.

04 · FileRoom — archive

The archival layer of Ascend, preserving finished assets and the resources behind them.

FileRoom keeps production assets, QR codes, translations, and other project materials together so they remain available for the next job.

05 · The Portal — composed

The front door to Ascend: one place to access the tools, projects, and resources behind the creative operation.

The Portal brings ArtStart, CopyDesk, CodeDesk, and FileRoom together, giving the team one place to start a job and find what they need to finish it.

Creative operations

Creative operations

Every graphics job has the visible creative work, which gets the attention — and then everything required to get it correctly into the world. The second part is easy to underestimate. It is also where a great deal of expensive failure lives: a tradeshow wall printed at 72 dpi is not a lesser creative problem. It is a large, expensive mistake.

Specifications, translations, versioning, asset preparation, delivery, and archiving are not ancillary to the work. They are part of making the work.

Ascend moves that work forward. ArtStart structures the job before it reaches the artist. CopyDesk establishes a canonical source before translation. CodeDesk handles production details like QR codes. FileRoom preserves the resulting work. The Portal brings it together.

The point isn’t to automate creative judgment. It’s to remove the operational work that competes with it, and to make the things that need to be precise more precise by giving them the structure and attention they require.

The visible benefit is time. The real benefit is attention.

The Ward

A complete product, conceived and built from the ground up.

A Ward is a real neighborhood. The one running above is Lafayette Square in St. Louis. Visit Lafayette Square →

The place is separate from the product

Which layer of The Ward to load

The Slab establishes the physical neighborhood; the Ward brings its civic and social layers online. The composite shows the finished experience; use the switcher to isolate either layer.

I conceived The Ward, identified what it should do and why, developed its visual and interaction language, designed its underlying systems, built the tools required to make it repeatable, wrote it, branded it, and got an actual neighborhood running inside it.

The result is a digital presence for real neighborhoods: a working platform that brings the physical place together with the people, places, businesses, events, exchanges, and local knowledge that make it a community.

Lafayette Square

The first Ward

Lafayette Square is the first Ward. Its living model contains 1,082 buildings and 5,146 trees — including the 775 trees of Lafayette Park, surveyed individually and representing 92 species. Nearly every building carries a historic record, while the Society Pages map the neighborhood’s restaurants, shops, parks, arts and cultural sites, services, community institutions and residences. Restaurants carry menus; Places can carry hours, events, photographs, history and reviews. Weather, daylight and the night sky keep the whole neighborhood situated in the actual place and time.

The Ward is repeatable because each neighborhood is authored through a set of purpose-built tools:

ExtentEstablishes the geographic bounds of the neighborhood
SurveyorBuilds the physical record of streets, parcels, buildings and landmarks
DesignerAuthors the neighborhood’s visual language
StageControls light, atmosphere, depth, color and post-production
PreviewGPU emulator for testing the finished environment
ArboristManages species, individual trees and seasonal foliage
MeteorologistAuthors clouds, weather and atmospheric conditions
Universal PlayerBrings the environment and civic layers together at runtime

WLVX

The things in the video became the interface.

WLVX was an interactive video platform I founded and led for nine years. It allowed publishers to make the objects moving through finished video interactive without covering the picture in conventional buttons, hotspots or interface chrome.

How it worked

Everything was designable

The things in a video could remain themselves rather than being reduced to buttons, boxes or hotspots. A viewer might select an object as it moved through the picture and see that same object collected in a Dock designed for the experience.

Behind the finished video ran a synchronized Object Map. WLVX used it to identify the objects in the picture and follow their exact shapes as they moved from frame to frame. Each could respond to a click or touch, change visually, be collected for later, or open something else.

What happened next was up to the publisher. An object could lead to a product, an article, a 3D model, a technical resource or an experience built specifically for it. Objects could also interact with one another, which made the same system useful for training and instruction as well as commerce and editorial work.

Because WLVX knew which objects were present, where they appeared and how people interacted with them, it could also produce information particular to the content itself: what drew attention, what was acted on, where objects appeared in the frame and how densely products populated the picture.

As founder and CEO, I led the product from concept through development: defining requirements and feature specifications, maintaining the roadmap and product backlog, and directing engineering and creative teams through development and deployment.

For two years, I worked as assistant to the Executive Director of the Provincetown Business Guild and, in practice, handled its design. It was a very small operation producing a remarkable amount of printed material: visitor guides, maps, event materials and the publications that connected visitors with the town’s businesses and places.

In print

Because we made the guides and maps ourselves, improving the design quickly became a matter of improving the system behind them. Business listings had to be maintained and organized; maps had to correspond to them; events, photography and local information had to fit around both.

When I arrived, the business directory was a section within a largely editorial visitor guide. As I worked on the publications, the listings increasingly became their organizing structure—the spine around which the rest of the material could be built.

Picture Wrap

When the last surviving maker of a picture dies, the picture wraps again.

Live at picture-wrap.com · posting closed pictures on Bluesky at @picture-wrap.social

Picture Wrap is a living archive of film and television and the people who made them. What counts as a picture—and who counts as one of its makers—is part of the project.

It follows each known maker through time; as people die, they move below the line, until no recorded member of the production remains.

“Known” matters. Older pictures often have incomplete records: many contributors, above and below the line, were never credited or have disappeared from history. Picture Wrap documents what can be established from the record that remains.

Building it meant reconciling production, biographical and image data from multiple sources with different licenses and attribution requirements, while exploring how a predominantly textual information system could still be beautiful.

Wrap Bot watches for new wraps, publishing the artwork and the person whose death closed the picture.

Methods & sources

Picture Wrap assembles its record from several independent sources rather than relying on a single film database. Filmographies, credits, biographical records, dates and images each have different strengths, gaps and rules governing how they can be used.

That made provenance part of the product. Data and media had to remain traceable to their sources while respecting different licenses, attribution requirements and terms of use. In some cases the distinction extends all the way to presentation: required credits, marks and language have to survive intact without feeling bolted onto the interface.

The historical record also sets a limit on what the project can claim. Particularly for older films, surviving credits are incomplete and many participants were never recorded at all. A Picture Wrap therefore marks the death of the last known surviving maker represented by the available record—not necessarily the last person who actually worked on the film.

Where possible, corrections are directed back to the public source rather than maintained as private fixes inside Picture Wrap, so an improved record can benefit everyone using that data downstream.

Scale Machine

Learn the scales. Play what you feel.

Visit Scale Machine →

I built Scale Machine to sharpen my scales so I could play more freely and improvisationally. For a transposing instrument, the notes you read depend on the instrument you play, so Scale Machine handles the translation automatically—generating properly spelled, engraved notation across ten scale types and all twelve keys for your instrument.

Turn on Listen and Scale Machine follows you note by note through the microphone, repeating a scale or advancing as you play.

Where it is going

Scale Machine is moving from practicing scales toward using them to play actual music. Enter a song and it will identify a small palette of scales that gets you inside it, already transposed and notated for your instrument. Rather than pretending every song has a single definitive key, it can account for music that moves, modulates, sits between major and minor, or needs more than one scale to describe what’s happening.

A companion Bluesky feed will periodically pick a song, show what its key means for different instruments, and link directly into Scale Machine ready to practice.
